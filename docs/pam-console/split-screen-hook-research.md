# Hooks for the split-screen explainer: the research

**For:** the Marrs Attacks social media agent
**Written:** 13 September 2026, from the research Marrs ran in September 2026 and the console built from it (decisions D102 to D105 and D112)
**Scope:** Marrs Attacks only. The Polynize account is a different game and these rules do not transfer; see the last section.

---

## 1. The one sentence

**The question is the product. Everything downstream is execution.**

A split-screen explainer is sixty seconds long and the title is the whole bet. If the title does not make a stranger stop, nothing about the script, the object on the screen or the timer matters, because nobody sees them. This document is about writing that title.

## 2. What the hook is, on the screen

The title does three jobs at once, and it is the same words in all three places:

| Where | What the viewer meets |
|---|---|
| On the screen, before Marrs walks on | The title, in the house type. The written hook. |
| The first spoken line | A fixed intro, always the same: "In under 60 seconds I'm going to explain to you". It stops there. |
| The second spoken line | The title, word for word. This is the hook. It opens with Why or How. |

The intro never carries the why or the how. The title is the hook, and the same title is the question on the first slide of the presentation the viewer sees. No paraphrase anywhere: the on-screen words, the spoken words and the caption's first line are identical.

Three hooks operate at once: visual (the walk-on with the timer), spoken (the title), written (the title on screen). Only the title is written by hand; the other two are the format.

## 3. Two shapes only

Every title is one of these. Anything else is not this format.

**Contradiction.** A claim the viewer cannot explain and suspects is wrong. Opens with **Why**.
Scored 9 or 10: *Why you need a 100-year plan to hit your goals. Why AI won't make your kids stupid. Why you trust AI most when it's wrong. Why your best ideas come on the toilet.*

**Wanted outcome.** Something the viewer wants and does not have. Opens with **How to**, and names an **outcome**, never a method.
Scored 9 or 10: *How to know when something's finished. How to make your work impossible to copy. How to have the best idea in the room. How to know what you're actually good at.*

A how-title that names a method fails. *How to plan your life backwards from 100* scored 5 (a method). Marrs rewrote the same idea as *Why you need a 100-year plan to hit your goals* and scored it 10. When a method is the only framing you can find, convert it to a why.

## 4. The killer test

**If the viewer can answer the title themselves, kill it.**

Recognition is not curiosity. An accurate description of the viewer's own behaviour reads as true, produces a nod, and produces no click. This is the single most common failure and it produces titles that are well written and worthless.

Failed on this test: *Why the last 10 percent takes longer than the first 90. Why you'd rather start something new than finish this. Why your first idea is never your best one.* All accurate. All closed.

Passed: *Why you abandon things at 90 percent.* Same territory, but the number makes a mystery instead of restating a fact.

**Never describe. Contradict them, or give them something.**

## 5. Construction rules

These are Marrs's rules, in his words, from seven rounds of scoring.

| Rule | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Focus anchor** | Every title needs a domain: AI, creativity, productivity, purpose, work. Never fully open. "Why you're further along than you think" fails, there is no category |
| **Borrowed vocabulary** | Use only words people actually use about themselves. Invented verbs kill titles. *Rot your brain* works because people say it. *Flatten, hollow out, melt, wreck* all failed |
| **Commonest phrasing** | Use the most natural form, not the second most. *Come in the shower* beats *arrive in the shower*. *Keeping you up at night* beats *keeping you awake* |
| **Behaviour, not feeling** | Name something observable. *Abandon at 90 percent* works. *Feel guilty about AI* instructs an emotion the viewer may not have |
| **Name the accusation** | Use the exact label people are given: *too old, cheating, stupid, lazy*. Abstractions fail: *fraud, crutch, the author* |
| **One idea, no second read** | If parsing is required, it is dead. Ambiguity scores 5 or below every time. Length is not the constraint: a twelve-word title scored 8 and a ten-word title scored 10. Short is usually the result of one clean idea, not the cause of one |
| **Concrete over abstract** | Abstract nouns produce 8s. Concrete images produce 10s. *Taste, decision, idea, question* score well. *Kids, the toilet, the room, 100 years, 90 percent* score higher. When a title is landing at 8, look for the concrete version of the same claim. This correlates strongly but does not govern: *How to know when something's finished* scored 10 with nothing concrete in it |
| **Title is the promise, not the twist** | The clever inversion lives in beat 2 as the reward for staying. *Why AI won't make you lazy. It'll make you exposed* is two titles. Cut at the full stop |
| **Relief over shame** | Shame hooks get watched. Relief hooks get sent to a specific person. Shareability is the primary objective, so default to relief and treat shame framings as deliberate tests |
| **News is a trigger, never the subject** | A news event enters at beat 1 of the script and never appears in the title. The title stays durable for months, and an underperforming piece can be re-pegged rather than rewritten |

**These tests apply to Marrs's own ideas too.** A Marrs-originated title scored 5 on fresh eyes in testing. The test is objective and overrides taste, including his.

## 6. The mechanical tests

Five things a title can fail without anyone's judgement. The console runs these on every title, and so should you before you spend a minute of taste on one:

1. Opens with **Why** or **How**. Anything else is not one of the two shapes.
2. **One sentence.** A full stop with words after it is two titles. Cut at the full stop.
3. **No double negative.** *Why you're not using AI wrong* needs a second read and fails.
4. **No question mark.** The title is a statement of the promise, not a question to the viewer.
5. **No em dash.** House rule, everywhere.

## 7. Where the words come from: the research arm

**The research does not find topics. It finds vocabulary.**

Angles come from Marrs and from the rules above. Research supplies two things only:

1. **Native phrasing.** The exact nouns and verbs people use about themselves.
2. **Saturation check.** Whether a framing is already worn out.

**Method**

- Search **first-person stems**, never topics: *"is AI making me", "am I the only one", "should I feel bad that", "is it cheating to", "I can't seem to", "does anyone else"*.
- Keep it verbatim. Paraphrase destroys the asset. *"I don't know where AI fits in my organisation"* is a title. *"They wanted AI readiness guidance"* is nothing.
- Harvest complaints, not conclusions. The complaint is in the person's words. The article's conclusion is in the writer's.
- Store as a phrase bank: the verbatim string, the source, the date.

**Sources, ranked by confession density**

1. Blind, Reddit
2. Substack notes and comment threads
3. YouTube comments under AI launches and product announcements
4. LinkedIn comment threads

Comment sections under news events are the richest seam. They show the fear in native language, already ranked by engagement.

**News handling.** An event lands. Ask what an ordinary person fears or hopes about it. Search that as a first-person stem. Invert it. Test it against sections 3 to 6.

### 8e. What the drift shows

Three titles were scored high early and low later:

| Title | Early | Late |
|---|---|---|
| How to finish what you start | 10 | 3 |
| How to plan your life backwards from 100 | 8 | 5 |
| Why you're using AI wrong | approved | flagged as saturated |

All three are how-methods or saturated framings. **Method framings and worn tropes read well on first pass and fail on re-reading.** When a title scores well but is a method or a familiar trope, discount it rather than trusting the first score.

## 9. How the console uses this

So the agent knows what the machine already does and what is left to a person:

- **The title gate.** When a rough idea comes in, April is given sections 3 to 6 and the whole calibration set as examples, and asked for six titles that pass plus the ones she killed with the failed test named. Every title she keeps is then run through the mechanical tests in section 6; a failure moves it to the killed list.
- **The Hook library.** Gate 1 on the Marrs Attacks board shows three groups: **Your hooks** (titles Marrs saved or typed), **Scored hooks** (the 9s and 10s, then the 8s, from section 8, one click to use), and **Archived hooks** (used, struck through, with the date).
- **One hook per piece, locked.** A split-screen has exactly one title. Once chosen it is locked: it names the piece, it is the spoken hook, and it is the question on the first slide. Other candidates are saved back to the library, not attached.
- **Out of circulation on schedule.** A hook is archived when a post made from it is scheduled, not when a piece is started. An abandoned piece gives its hook back.
- **Two things not yet built.** The phrase bank from section 7 is a method for a person for now; the console does not harvest anything. And the focus anchor is shown on each proposed title but not yet stored.

## 10. General hook craft that still applies

Polynize's older hook guidance was written for a different audience and a different format, and most of it is superseded here by the calibration data. Four of its rules still hold and are worth keeping in mind as a second pass:

1. **Context-complete.** The title must make full sense to someone who has never heard of Marrs, Polynize or the topic. No jargon, no "this" or "it" pointing at something unseen.
2. **One open loop.** Exactly one question the piece then answers. Two ideas in a hook is zero hooks.
3. **Ownable.** If any creator could post the identical line, sharpen the point of view.
4. **The payoff is real.** The piece must deliver what the title promises. No bait.

Where these and section 8 disagree, section 8 wins. The data is his.

## 11. Do not use this for the Polynize account

The Marrs Attacks account is growth. The Polynize account is lead generation. Three rules invert, so none of this transfers without its own validation run:

1. **Relief becomes problem.** Marrs titles hand the viewer relief, because relief is what gets a video sent to a specific person. A Polynize buyer who feels reassured does not book a call. Polynize needs the viewer holding a problem they cannot unsee.
2. **The last beat changes job.** For Marrs it is a free action the viewer takes tonight, and the video is complete in itself. For Polynize it must open the gap the lead magnet fills, without becoming an ad.
3. **The vocabulary moves.** Consumer confessions on Reddit and Substack do not contain the language of a Head of L&D or a security awareness lead. That corpus is client call transcripts and LinkedIn threads.
